    Both means a neighborhood faces serious flood risk and serious hardship at once. The plan calls these double vulnerability zones.

    The short version

    The problem

    A 2016 city estimate put the number of septic tanks needing replacement at about 65,000. Connecting the 35 priority areas on the city's list is estimated at $1.6 billion, so the city has to choose who goes first. It ranks them on a 100-point matrix, last updated in June 2026.

    The conflict

    Neighborhoods facing the worst flooding are often on the water. Neighborhoods facing the most hardship are often inland. Blend both into one score, as the top 10 list above does, and one group gets pushed down.

    The proposal

    Keep two separate lists, one for climate risk and one for hardship. Neighborhoods at the top of both go first, then the lists take turns. Each project is fully funded before the next begins.

    Why a rising water table is a problem

    Drag the slider to add rain and watch the water table rise. A septic drainfield needs dry soil beneath it to treat wastewater. A sewer line is sealed, so it keeps working.

          Terms the lab uses

          Climate risk score
          How exposed a neighborhood is to flooding, storm tides and rising water, using federal flood maps and 30-year projections.
          100-year floodplain
          Land with a 1 in 100 chance of flooding in any given year. It can still flood more than once a century.
          Base flood elevation
          How high floodwater is expected to rise. Lower ground is at greater risk.
          Hardship score
          How hard failing septic systems would hit residents, based on income, health and other vulnerabilities. Home value is deliberately left out so wealth is never mistaken for need.
          Social vulnerability index
          A federal CDC score from 0 to 1 that combines income, housing, age, language and other factors. Higher means more vulnerable.
          Double vulnerability zone
          A neighborhood in the top eight on both the climate risk list and the hardship list. It is funded first.
          Benefit score (CIS)
          Short for Composite Impact Score. It measures how much good a project does: properties and people served, failures fixed, cost per person and share of vulnerable residents.

          About this tool

          Plain-language glossary

          Septic tank phase-out
          Jacksonville's program to connect neighborhoods that use septic tanks to the city sewer system. It is governed by Chapter 751 of the city code.
          Property (parcel)
          One lot of land, usually a single home or business. The city counts projects in parcels.
          Fully funded
          One project gets all the money it needs to finish before the next one starts, instead of money being split across many projects that never finish.
          70% consent rule
          City ordinance requires 70% of property owners in a neighborhood to agree before work begins. This plan keeps that rule.
          City ranking
          The order the city uses today: a 100-point matrix that combines Duval County Health Department site scores with community factors such as development before 1968. Last updated June 2026.

          Terms about the scoring itself, such as climate risk, hardship and the benefit score, are explained in the County lab.

          Two frameworks, kept separate

          The City matrix (official) is the ranking the city and JEA use today. Its property counts, scores, ranks, health department site scores and costs come straight from the June 2026 matrix. The Septic Scope (proposed) framework is a separate idea: two lists, double vulnerability zones, full funding one project at a time, and a benefit score. Anything labeled with the yellow tag follows from the proposal and is not an official ranking.

          Where the Septic Scope framework came from

          Septic Scope is built on a policy brief proposing an amendment to Chapter 751 of the Jacksonville Code of Ordinances, which governs the Septic Tank Phase-Out program. The framework was developed by a Duval County student, reviewed with a University of North Florida dean, and presented to the Jacksonville Environmental Protection Board.

          How the Septic Scope lists work

          • A neighborhood is a double vulnerability zone when it ranks in the top eight on both the climate and equity scores. It goes first.
          • Everyone else joins the list where their score is higher, and the two lists alternate.
          • Median home value is not used, so wealth is never mistaken for need.
          • The 70% resident consent rule still applies to every project.
